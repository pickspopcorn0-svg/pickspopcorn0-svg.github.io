Why Indian Cinema Is Still
So Widely Misunderstood

It's not misunderstood because it lacks craft. It's misunderstood because most people meet it through clips, jokes, and viral moments instead of actual films.

Essay
Indian Cinema · Culture

How the reputation got built

Indian cinema is one of the oldest film industries in the world, one of the most productive, and one of the most watched. It still gets talked about globally like it's a curiosity. Outside India, real curiosity is rare. Instead, it gets reduced to the same few labels: loud, long, emotional, unrealistic, musical. Someone brings up the slow-motion action clips that went viral. Someone jokes about people dancing in the middle of a serious scene. Someone shrugs and treats the whole thing like a meme, as if a billion-plus people never quite learned how to tell stories properly.

The problem with this is not that the criticism exists. Plenty of Indian films are bad, and saying so is fine. Indian cinema is a huge ecosystem and like any huge ecosystem it has weak writing, lazy formulas, and films that deserve honest pushback. But there is a difference between critique and dismissal. Critique watches the full film. Dismissal watches five seconds and decides it has seen enough.

If you want to understand why the perception exists, and why it keeps coming back, you have to look at what actually travels online, and what stays behind.

The algorithm has a very specific taste

Indian films did not suddenly become exaggerated because of social media. Scale, heightened drama, and big gestures have always existed across many Indian industries, in different proportions and for different reasons. What changed is what the internet chose to export.

Short-form platforms reward shock value. A gravity-defying punch is more shareable than a quiet conversation. A mass entry moment gets clipped more often than a two-minute scene about grief or moral doubt. Over time, those fragments start standing in for the whole industry. It is not that Indian cinema is only spectacle. It is that spectacle survives the algorithm better than subtlety does.

This creates a specific kind of fake confidence in people who have never watched a full Indian film. They feel like they have seen enough. In reality they have seen seconds, usually from the loudest corners of commercial cinema, and the conclusion becomes automatic.

You are not judging Indian cinema. You are judging the algorithm's selection of it.

Spectacle itself is not the enemy here. When it is done well it is a strength, a deliberate style. RRR is the clearest recent example. It is big, dramatic, and unapologetically larger than life. It is also carefully designed, emotionally clear, and fully committed to its own world. The things people call "too much" are exactly what makes it work, because the filmmaking is confident enough that the scale feels earned rather than random. That is the distinction that gets lost in the five-second clip: the features people mock become a strength when the craft is actually there.

Why the action looks the way it does

Indian action cinema gets mocked for ignoring physics. That is not entirely wrong. Many films exaggerate strength, endurance, and scale far beyond realism. But the exaggeration is doing something. In several Indian storytelling traditions, heroes are not meant to feel like regular people. They represent ideas: resistance, justice, survival, dignity, the feeling of refusing to be pushed around. When a character overpowers ten enemies it is not a tactical demonstration. The fight is a statement.

You can dislike this and still recognise it is intentional. The real problem is when people treat the style as proof that Indian cinema is technically incapable or childish. Plenty of Indian films treat violence as ugly, frightening, and consequential. Plenty refuse the heroic fantasy entirely. Those films do not dominate the stereotype because restraint does not become a meme as quickly. A well-written scene of fear will not travel the way a loud slow-motion punch does.

Songs are not always interruptions

Nothing confuses outside audiences more than songs. The assumption runs like this: songs interrupt the story, so they are indulgent, so they are bad storytelling. It sounds clean, but it is not the full picture.

In Indian cinema, songs were never designed to behave like Western musical numbers. They often work like emotional chapters. They compress time. They externalise feelings that dialogue alone cannot carry. They can replace scenes rather than pause them, functioning like a montage, a confession, a memory, or a break in reality. The film is not always stopping to decorate itself. Often it is using a different tool to move forward.

Yes, there are films where songs feel forced and tone collapses because a track was inserted for commercial reasons. That is a valid criticism. But the best films do not use songs as wallpaper. And the assumption that Indian cinema equals musical cinema is increasingly outdated. Many contemporary Indian films remove songs entirely, especially in realist and independent spaces. These films rarely reach people who already decided Indian cinema is not for them, because the stereotype blocks curiosity before it begins.

Big emotion is not automatically shallow

Indian films are often accused of melodrama, and the accusation usually carries an implication: subtlety equals maturity, bigger emotion means childish storytelling. That is a narrow way to judge anything.

Melodrama is not a failure of intelligence. It is an emotional style. Indian cinema often asks audiences to feel first and think later. Emotions are placed in the open, sometimes amplified, because the experience is meant to be communal. A theatre full of people reacting together is part of the language, not a sign that the film cannot do anything else.

When it works, this approach delivers something a lot of modern "cool and detached" filmmaking genuinely struggles with: honest emotion without irony. When it does not work, the criticism is fair. But a better question than "why is it so emotional" is "what is this emotion doing?" Sometimes it is catharsis. Sometimes it is protest. Sometimes it is grief that has nowhere else to go.

Films that make the stereotype difficult to hold

You do not need a hundred films to change the perception. A handful is enough. These are not exceptions that prove Indian cinema is occasionally capable of depth. They are proof that it is a much wider landscape than the loudest exports suggest.

01
Sardar Udham (2021)
Quiet. Political. Controlled. Devastating.

Restrained in a way that surprises people who come in with expectations. It refuses spectacle even when it could easily exploit it. Violence here is not thrilling, it is traumatic. Grief is not romanticised, it is suffocating. The film does not beg for applause. It builds pressure slowly and trusts the audience to sit inside discomfort without being told what to feel.

If someone's only image of Indian cinema is loudness and instant gratification, this film is a direct answer. The emotion is heavy precisely because it is controlled. It does not turn history into easy heroism.

02
Dangal (2016)
Grounded emotion. Earned triumph.

Often discussed like it is just a sports film, but the reason it hits is the discipline underneath the drama. It is built on training, failure, repetition, and the slow grind of earning confidence. The triumphs feel satisfying because the film made you watch the cost of them.

It also shows that mainstream Indian cinema can be grounded without becoming cold, and widely loved without being shallow. Those two things are not usually considered possible from the outside.

03
The Lunchbox (2013)
Minimal. Quiet. Precise.

No big speeches, no dramatic twists, no emotional manipulation. It builds intimacy through small routines and quiet loneliness, through the strange comfort of being understood by someone you have never met. The writing is controlled, the film respects silence, and the emotions accumulate slowly rather than arriving announced.

It does not go viral because its power is cumulative. But sit with it and you feel exactly how precise it is. This is where the "Indian cinema is only loud" idea falls apart.

04
Dhurandhar
Mainstream Indian cinema done properly.

Fully mainstream, and proof of what happens when the elements people complain about are handled with actual control. Songs that do not feel like interruptions. Emotion that is earned. Scale that serves the story rather than overwhelming it. Even at around three and a half hours it holds, because everything is mixed properly.

It did not reject Indian cinema's identity to look global. It refined the identity and used it better. That is the actual argument for why Indian cinema does not need to become something else to be great.

Why the misunderstanding stays

Because stereotypes are convenient. Because the internet exports what shocks faster than what it explains. Because it is easier to laugh at five seconds than to sit through two hours and actually understand tone, context, and intention. And because once a joke becomes the default opinion, people stop looking for anything that might challenge it.

Indian cinema is messy, ambitious, inconsistent, experimental, commercial, artistic, regional, global, traditional, modern, and always evolving. It contains brilliant films and terrible films, like every major cinema culture on earth. The difference is that it is rarely encountered on its own terms. What travels fastest is noise, not context. What goes viral is not intention.

Watch it fully. Patiently. The stereotype does not survive long.

This essay talks about patterns, not absolutes. Indian cinema spans many industries, languages, and audiences. If one corner does not work for you, it does not mean the whole ecosystem is the same. It usually means you were shown the loudest sample first.
