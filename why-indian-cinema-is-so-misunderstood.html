Why Indian Cinema Is So Misunderstood

Indian cinema gets judged by viral fragments. This is the full picture, with the good, the unfair, and the truths in between.

Indian cinema is bigger than the cliché

Indian cinema is often reduced to a barrage of clichés in the global imagination. Mention “Bollywood,” and many outsiders envision three-hour extravaganzas packed with over-the-top action sequences, colorful musical numbers, and melodramatic plots. Despite being one of the world’s largest and most prolific film industries, Indian cinema remains deeply misunderstood. Stereotypes have obscured its true scale, diversity, and artistry. In reality, those familiar tropes – the gravity-defying stunts, the spontaneous song-and-dance routines, the operatic emotions – are just one facet of a rich cinematic tapestry. To appreciate this complexity, we must examine where the common criticisms come from, which contain a grain of truth, and how they miss the bigger picture of India’s film culture.

More Than Just Over-the-Top Action

One of the most prevalent misconceptions is that Indian films are nothing but absurd, logic-defying action flicks. It’s true that mainstream Indian cinema (especially commercial masala films) loves its larger-than-life heroes and physics-defying stunts. In the Telugu-language industry, for example, filmmakers are renowned for crafting spectacular fight scenes – high-octane sequences often edited for maximal impact. These can become viral sensations on the internet, reinforcing the idea that Indian action is inherently “over the top.” Audiences see clips of a hero single-handedly vanquishing a dozen goons or sliding a horse under a truck, and assume all Indian films trade subtlety for spectacle.

Yet this is a skewed view. For every bombastic action blockbuster, there are countless Indian films that favor realism and narrative depth over stunts. The prevalence of outsized action is partly cultural: Indian popular storytelling has long embraced mythic imagination. In a country raised on epic tales of warriors and gods, viewers readily accept a hero who performs superhuman feats. As one observer noted, a rural Indian audience might find Hollywood’s sci-fi fantasies like Avatar relatively mundane – flying creatures and superpowers are familiar tropes from Hindu epics – whereas seeing a beloved Bollywood star dancing on the Brooklyn Bridge feels like true fantasy. What seems “excessive” in one culture may be cathartic entertainment in another.

Indeed, when S. S. Rajamouli’s Telugu epic RRR (2022) exploded into global consciousness, Western viewers celebrated its unabashedly over-the-top style. The film’s grand spectacle – larger-than-life characters engaging in outrageous action sequences and exuberant musical numbers – captured international attention. Critics dubbed it a “global superhero film,” and audiences in New York’s theaters cheered and gasped at the sheer audacity of the on-screen heroics. Some first-time viewers marveled that RRR felt like “watching six films in one,” mixing action, drama, bromance, comedy, and musical elements into a single heady brew. In other words, the very qualities once mocked as excessive were embraced as a breath of fresh air.

The lesson is that Indian cinema’s penchant for spectacle has its own context and intent. Yes, logic takes a backseat at times, but the goal is to deliver maximum entertainment and emotional payoff. These films are tapping into a tradition of heroic storytelling and communal viewing experience, where clapping and whistling at the hero’s feats is part of the fun. And importantly, not all Indian action cinema is escapist fantasy – filmmakers also create gritty, realistic thrillers and dramas when the story calls for it. Dismissing Indian cinema as only “over-the-top action” misses the careful calibration behind its madness and the variety of tones it actually spans.

Music and Dance as Storytelling, Not Fluff

In the global eye, another enduring image of Indian films is the song-and-dance routine – joyful dancers materializing out of nowhere, lavishly choreographed numbers interrupting the plot. It’s an understandable stereotype: for decades, popular Hindi cinema routinely inserted multiple musical sequences into its narratives. These sequences, bursting with vivid costumes and catchy tunes, can feel jarringly alien to viewers used to Hollywood’s more straightforward storytelling. But music in Indian cinema is not mere excess – it is integral to the storytelling tradition.

Historically, Indian films evolved from indigenous theater forms that heavily featured music, dance, and mythology. Early filmmakers recognized that songs could transcend language barriers in a nation of many tongues, conveying emotion to audiences who might not all be literate. In fact, the dominance of musical numbers in early Bollywood partly arose because a largely illiterate populace could still connect with a film through its songs and spectacle. The first Indian talkie, Alam Ara (1931), famously featured seven songs, heralding a trend that would define Indian cinema for generations. Incorporating musical interludes became a hallmark – an expected feature that audiences came to cherish.

Crucially, these songs often serve a narrative purpose. In Indian filmmaking, it’s common to use a song as a vehicle for a character’s inner emotions or to advance a plot thread in a montage. A longing for love might be expressed not in dialogue but in a soulful duet; a village’s joy might erupt in a big dance number. One commentator astutely noted that every Indian film seeks moments of visual or emotional spectacle – sometimes it’s a fight scene or a sweeping landscape, but very often it’s a song, functioning like a poetic crescendo of feeling. In other words, songs are how Indian cinema “shows” rather than “tells” intense emotion.

It’s also a mistake to think all Indian films are musicals. The stereotype persists that every Bollywood movie will halt for a dance sequence, but in truth many modern Indian films – especially outside mainstream Hindi cinema – forego song-and-dance entirely. Even within Bollywood, the past two decades have seen a shift: filmmakers often use songs more judiciously, as background snippets or single montages, rather than the five or six full production numbers of yesteryear. Moreover, India’s thriving art-house and indie films prove that silence can speak as loudly as songs. Regional cinemas too have produced restrained, song-free narratives when appropriate. The bottom line is that music in Indian cinema is a tool – one that can be flamboyant or subtle – but it’s employed with intent.

Melodrama, Mythology, and Mastery in Storytelling

Perhaps the broadest critique leveled at Indian films is that they suffer from poor storytelling – that they’re melodramatic, simplistic, or technically inferior. Critics point to melodrama that borders on soap opera, convenient coincidences, or plots that juggle tones haphazardly. There is some truth here: mainstream Indian cinema has historically favored a more is more approach to narrative. Emotions are often worn on the sleeve; a family reunion might cue swelling strings and tears, a villain’s defeat might spur a bombastic monologue.

But labeling this as “poor storytelling” misses the cultural context. Indian popular cinema inherited a love for melodrama from older art forms – the ancient Sanskrit dramas and epic poems, the folk theater traditions, and the 19th-century Parsi theater that thrived on high emotion and moral narratives. These influences created a narrative style where heightened drama was not a flaw but a feature, meant to stir the collective heart of a diverse audience.

That said, it would be wildly incorrect to assume that Indian cinema lacks nuance or craft. In fact, India has produced some of world cinema’s most revered storytellers. The Bengali filmmaker Satyajit Ray is a prime example. Ray’s humane, quietly observant style proved that Indian narratives could be as sophisticated and understated as any.

The mid-20th century also saw India’s parallel cinema movement produce a treasure trove of critically acclaimed films. Visionary directors crafted works that tackled social realities with innovative style and depth. These films demonstrated a mastery of the cinematic form and thoroughly debunked the notion that Indian storytellers lack skill or originality.

Even within Bollywood, quality has always coexisted with the kitsch. The problem is one of perception and exposure. Western audiences often see a wider sample of Indian cinema than they do of other foreign cinemas. In contrast, many industries are represented abroad mainly by their greatest hits. So the comparison is often unfair from the start.

It’s also worth noting how Indian cinema has evolved over time. Filmmakers experiment with genre and trim excess when needed. You’ll find films that are raw and minimalist as often as ones that are flamboyant. The industry’s output is so vast that it encompasses the full spectrum from mindless to masterpiece.

One Nation, Many Cinemas Beyond “Bollywood”

A key to understanding Indian cinema’s misunderstood nature is recognizing its diversity. Internationally, people often use “Bollywood” as a blanket term for all Indian film. “Bollywood” specifically refers to the Hindi-language film industry centered in Mumbai. It is just one of many film industries in India.

The country produces films in over 20 languages each year, and several regional industries rival Bollywood in output and influence. Each of these industries has its own history, stars, stylistic conventions, and audience base. Together, they make India the world’s most prolific film-producing nation by far.

Understanding this patchwork is crucial. Many misconceptions arise from assuming one face of Indian cinema represents the whole. This massive scale means there’s no single formula. The range of genres and storytelling traditions within Indian cinema is staggering.

This diversity also extends to storytelling styles. Not all regions favor the Bollywood-style musical format. Some industries lean into particular genres. There’s cross-pollination too. A pan-Indian film culture is emerging, where collaborations span languages and a hit in one region can travel nationwide.

Conclusion: Beyond the Frames of Misconception

It’s easy to see how misconceptions persist. For decades, the most visible exports of Indian cinema were its most flamboyant products. They are memorable and culturally distinctive, but they are not the whole story.

What criticisms of Indian cinema are rooted in truth are usually matters of style rather than substance. Styles arise from culture. Dismissing Indian films for not mirroring Hollywood’s norms would mean overlooking the unique cinematic language they’ve developed.

As Indian cinema continues to globalize, understanding is slowly growing. The duality captures the essence of Indian cinema’s appeal. It can be bombastic and masala, and it can be subtle and artful, sometimes even in the same film.

In the final reckoning, to understand Indian cinema is to embrace a different perspective on what movies can do. Indian cinema is misunderstood not because it lacks merit, but because it’s been viewed through the wrong lens. Adjust the focus, look between the frames, and a new appreciation comes into view. The world of Indian films is vast and varied, and it’s ready to astonish anyone who cares to watch with an open mind.
