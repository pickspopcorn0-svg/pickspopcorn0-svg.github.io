Why Indian Cinema Is Still So Widely Misunderstood

Indian cinema carries a strange burden. It is one of the oldest film industries in the world, one of the most productive, and one of the most watched — yet it is often discussed as if it were a novelty. Outside India, it is rarely approached with curiosity. Instead, it is reduced to shorthand: loud, long, emotional, unrealistic, musical.

Mention Indian films in a global conversation and the response is predictable. Someone brings up slow-motion action clips that went viral. Someone jokes about people dancing in the middle of a serious scene. Someone dismisses the entire industry with a shrug, as if a billion-plus people somehow never learned how to tell stories properly.

What makes this misunderstanding frustrating is not that criticism exists — criticism is healthy — but that the criticism is built almost entirely on fragments. Clips without context. Scenes without setup. Moments divorced from intention. Indian cinema is judged by what escapes its frame, not by what actually lives inside it.

To understand why this perception exists, and why it persists, you have to look at what Indian cinema really is — not just what travels the fastest online.

The Viral Clip Problem: How Spectacle Became the Whole Story

Indian films didn’t suddenly become exaggerated because of social media. They always had scale, emotion, and heightened drama. What changed is what the internet chose to export.

Short-form platforms reward shock value. A gravity-defying punch is more shareable than a quiet conversation. A mass action moment gets clipped more often than a two-minute scene about grief or moral doubt. Over time, these fragments begin to stand in for the whole.

This creates a false pattern. People who have never watched a full Indian film feel confident judging them. They have “seen enough.” In reality, they’ve seen seconds.

Yes, Indian mainstream cinema — especially in some commercial industries — embraces spectacle unapologetically. But spectacle is not absence of craft. It is a language choice. Indian popular cinema has always leaned into mythic exaggeration, where heroes are closer to folk legends than everyday realism. This doesn’t mean every Indian film follows that logic. It means some choose to, deliberately.

The mistake is assuming that this is the only mode Indian cinema knows.

Why the Action Feels “Too Much” — and Why That’s Not the Full Picture

Indian action cinema often gets mocked for ignoring physics. That criticism isn’t entirely wrong. Many films do exaggerate strength, endurance, and scale beyond realism. But this exaggeration comes from a cultural lineage that values symbolism over simulation.

In Indian storytelling traditions, heroes aren’t meant to feel like regular people. They represent ideas: resistance, justice, survival, dignity. When a character overpowers ten enemies, it’s not a tactical demonstration — it’s a visual metaphor.

That said, equating Indian cinema with only this kind of action ignores a massive body of work that actively rejects it.

There are Indian films where violence is clumsy, painful, and frightening. Where heroes lose, hesitate, break down. Where action is grounded not in fantasy but in consequence. These films don’t go viral because restraint doesn’t scream for attention.

They exist anyway.

Songs, Interruptions, and the Most Misread Storytelling Tool

Nothing confuses non-Indian audiences more than songs.

The assumption is simple: songs interrupt the story. Therefore, they are indulgent. Therefore, they are bad storytelling.

But in Indian cinema, songs were never meant to behave like Western musical numbers. They function closer to emotional chapters. They compress time. They externalise feelings that dialogue cannot. They often replace scenes rather than pause them.

Yes, there are films where songs feel misplaced. Yes, there are moments where tone collapses because of forced musical insertions. Indian cinema has struggled with this balance for decades, and criticism here is valid.

But the best Indian films don’t use songs as decoration. They use them as narrative devices.

And importantly, many Indian films don’t use songs at all.

The idea that Indian cinema equals musical cinema is outdated. Contemporary Indian films — especially in realist and independent spaces — often remove songs entirely. Silence, ambient sound, and minimal score take precedence. These films rarely reach people who already decided Indian cinema isn’t for them.

Melodrama Isn’t Immaturity — It’s a Different Emotional Contract

Indian films are often accused of being melodramatic. The accusation usually comes with the implication that subtlety equals maturity.

But melodrama is not a lack of intelligence. It is an emotional style. Indian cinema traditionally asks its audience to feel first, analyse later. Emotions are not hidden. They are placed in the open, exaggerated at times, because the experience is meant to be communal.

This approach doesn’t always work. Some films overdo it. Some manipulate rather than earn emotion. But when it works, it creates something Western cinema often avoids — emotional honesty without irony.

Indian cinema’s relationship with melodrama comes from theatre, mythology, and oral storytelling. It was never built for minimalist distance. Judging it solely by Western narrative restraint misses the point of what it is trying to do.

Films That Break the Stereotype Completely

If someone believes Indian cinema is only loud, unrealistic, and shallow, they simply haven’t seen enough of it. A handful of films is enough to dismantle that perception.

Sardar Udham (2021)

This film is quiet, restrained, and devastating. It refuses spectacle even when it could easily exploit it. Violence is not thrilling here — it’s traumatic. Grief is not romanticised — it’s suffocating.

Sardar Udham doesn’t explain itself to the audience. It trusts them to sit with discomfort. It proves that Indian cinema can be patient, political, and emotionally restrained without losing impact.
