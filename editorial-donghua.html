My Favorite Donghua

Seven cultivation series worth the long investment. These are not gateway picks chosen to be safe. They are the ones that stayed.

7 Picks Cultivation Progression Ancient Worlds Power Systems

The first time I tried to get into donghua I bounced off it completely. The pacing was slower than I expected and nobody I knew was watching it so I had no one to ask whether it was worth continuing. I gave up and came back a year later, and then I could not stop.

The thing that nobody tells you upfront is that donghua rewards a completely different kind of attention than most animation. You are not supposed to watch it the way you watch a 12-episode anime series where everything is tight and deliberately paced. Donghua moves like a novel. It builds the world before it builds the tension. It introduces the rules before it breaks them. The payoffs take a long time to arrive, and when they do, they land with a weight that shorter series cannot manufacture because they never had the space to set it up properly.

These seven series are the ones that got me past the initial resistance and kept me watching long after I stopped thinking of myself as someone who watches donghua. They are all different from each other in tone and approach, but they all have one thing in common: at some point while watching them, I genuinely stopped wanting to do anything else.

01 of 07

Soul Land

Cultivation · Found Family · Destiny · Rebirth

Something happens around episode thirty of Soul Land where you stop tracking the plot and start caring about the people. That shift is not accidental. The series spends its early episodes building a world and a set of rules with unusual patience, and by the time the Shrek Academy arc arrives, you have enough investment in Tang San and his team that their competition losses hurt and their wins feel genuinely earned rather than scripted.

The core premise puts Tang San in a strange position from the start. He carries the memories and techniques of his previous life as the greatest Tang Sect master of his generation, but in this new world, those techniques are classified as hidden weapons and are explicitly forbidden. He cannot simply be what he was. He has to rebuild within the rules of a system that would punish him for showing what he actually knows, which means every early display of ability is a carefully calculated risk. Watching him manage that pressure while also being a twelve-year-old trying to survive academy life gives the series an unusual kind of tension beneath its surface.

The spirit ring system deserves specific attention because it is one of the most consequential world-building decisions in cultivation donghua. When a spirit master grows strong enough, they hunt spirit beasts in the forest and absorb a ring from a kill. The ring they absorb determines a new ability permanently. There is no undoing a ring choice. A ring from a weak beast limits your ceiling forever. A ring from a beast too strong and you die. The calculation that goes into each hunt, and the consequences that follow each choice, give the series a texture that most cultivation stories skip entirely by making power upgrades feel automatic.

The team around Tang San is the other reason Soul Land holds together as well as it does across its enormous runtime. Xiao Wu, Oscar, Dai Mubai, Ning Rongrong, Zhu Zhuqing, and Ma Hongjun each have their own cultivation limits, their own family pressures, their own reasons for being there. The series gives each of them real arcs rather than treating them as backdrop for Tang San's progression. During the Spirit Fighting competitions, you find yourself genuinely uncertain about outcomes because the show has convinced you that any of these characters could lose, and that losing would actually matter to them.

The romantic thread between Tang San and Xiao Wu is handled with more care than you typically see in this genre. It develops slowly enough that it feels like a real relationship forming rather than a plot requirement being fulfilled. When the series finally reveals the full truth of what Xiao Wu is and what their connection means, it recontextualizes everything you watched before it and gives the final stretch of the series its emotional backbone. I did not expect to be as affected by the ending as I was. I probably should have, given how long the series spent preparing for it.

There is a ceiling on this series that very few others in the genre reach. Start here if you are new to cultivation donghua. Stay for the team, the rings, and whatever the series does to you in the final arc.

02 of 07

Battle Through the Heavens

Underdog · Revenge · Power Progression · Clan Politics

There is a specific kind of satisfaction that Battle Through the Heavens is engineered to deliver, and it delivers it better than almost any other cultivation story I have seen. The series opens with Xiao Yan at his lowest. He was a prodigy. Something took that away. Now his fiancée is standing in front of his clan announcing she is ending their engagement, and the people around him either pity him or despise him for wasting his bloodline. The series does not rush past this. It holds you inside his humiliation long enough that the anger it builds becomes personal.

Most cultivation stories use a difficult starting point as a launching pad they move away from quickly. Battle Through the Heavens keeps returning to it. New enemies appear who represent the same contempt Xiao Yan faced at the beginning. New situations put him back in the position of being underestimated and dismissed. Each time, the circumstances are different enough that it does not feel repetitive, and each time his response has evolved in a way that reflects how much has changed since the last time someone looked through him. The series is unusually good at using its villain and rival characters not just as obstacles but as mirrors.

Yao Chen, the ancient soul sealed inside a ring Xiao Yan inherits, is the relationship that gives the series its emotional core. He is centuries old, immensely powerful in his sealed state, and deeply unwilling to waste his time on someone who cannot meet his standards. The early dynamic between them is almost combative. Yao Chen has seen gifted cultivators squander everything and ordinary ones surprise him, and he is not immediately convinced Xiao Yan is worth the investment. Watching him become convinced, gradually and against his own skepticism, is one of the better character dynamics in the genre.

The cultivation economy in Battle Through the Heavens is worth understanding because it shapes everything that happens. Rare pills and ingredients are the currency of power. Sects and clans that control access to them control the distribution of strength itself. Xiao Yan, who has no family backing powerful enough to give him resources, has to earn or steal or outmaneuver his way to every material he needs. This gives his progression a scrappy, improvised quality that feels distinct from heroes who move through their worlds with institutional support behind them.

The alchemy system is also developed in genuine depth. Pill-making in this world is a form of combat, a skill that takes as much cultivation talent as fighting. Some of the most tense sequences in the series are Xiao Yan racing to produce a specific pill under pressure, managing temperature and ingredients and timing while people wait on the outcome. When he becomes a true alchemist, it opens strategic possibilities that pure combat strength cannot, and the series uses that to keep him interesting even when his fighting power is outmatched.

03 of 07

Perfect World

Mythic Scale · Ancient Realms · Bloodline Wars · Cosmic Lore

Perfect World starts in a place so small it almost feels like a trick. A child in a remote village learns to hunt. The village has a stone cauldron with patterns carved into it that nobody alive understands. The elders are cautious about the forest nearby. Nothing about this beginning suggests the scale of what the series eventually becomes, which is part of why the later revelations hit as hard as they do.

Shi Hao carries a bloodline so rare and so coveted that ancient clans have been waiting across generations for someone like him to appear. He does not know this for a long time. He grows up thinking he is simply a village child with unusual physical gifts, and the series takes real time letting you know him at that stage before the larger world discovers he exists. When it does, the series shifts into something altogether more dangerous. The forces that begin to take interest in him have resources and patience that make his village-level strength feel almost comically inadequate, and the series never pretends otherwise.

The worldbuilding in Perfect World operates on a geological timescale. There are divine clans in this series that have existed for so long their origins are genuinely mythological. Ancient battles left permanent marks on the landscape. Techniques sealed inside ruins carry the memory of practitioners who died thousands of years before Shi Hao was born. The present feels like the surface of something much older, and the series uses that depth to make even ordinary conflicts feel weighted with history.

Shi Hao's cultivation path is unconventional in a way that causes him constant problems. He develops along a line that more established cultivators do not recognize, which means they consistently underestimate him right until the moment they cannot. That asymmetry runs throughout the series and gives many of the confrontation sequences a satisfying quality where the outcome is genuinely uncertain because neither side is working with accurate information about the other.

The supporting characters in Perfect World are stronger than in most cultivation donghua. His companions from the academy have their own bloodlines, their own clan pressures, their own relationship with the ancient world Shi Hao is only beginning to understand. Some of them come from the very families that would traditionally view someone like him as a threat or a resource. Watching those relationships form and then get tested as the stakes increase is one of the series' most consistent pleasures.

Perfect World is demanding viewing. It expects you to track a large number of factions, historical references, and cultivation concepts without always stopping to explain them. Viewers who give it that attention will find one of the most fully realized worlds in the genre. Viewers who want something more accessible might find the density exhausting. Worth knowing which one you are before committing.

04 of 07

Shrouding the Heavens

Atmospheric · Deep Lore · Ancient Mysteries · Cosmic Horror

Shrouding the Heavens opens on the moon. A group of astronauts discovers ancient bronze coffins arranged in a deliberate pattern on the lunar surface. The inscriptions are in classical Chinese. Nobody can explain what they are doing there or how long they have been there. The series opens on this image and then spends its entire run following the chain of questions it creates, which turn out to lead somewhere far older and stranger than anyone standing next to those coffins could have guessed.

This framing gives Shrouding the Heavens a completely different texture from other cultivation donghua. Ye Fan does not have ambitions. He has questions. He stumbles into cultivation not because he wants power but because the things he is investigating require him to survive in places where only cultivators can go. His path through the series is driven by curiosity more than competition, which is unusual in a genre where almost every protagonist is defined by a specific goal they are working toward.

The world itself is the most unsettling thing in the series. There are ancient sites in Shrouding the Heavens where the ground feels wrong, where ruins suggest events so catastrophic that the entire era they came from has been erased from collective memory. The series keeps implying that something happened, a long time ago, that nobody living now understands, and that the cultivation world people inhabit in the present is built on top of that unknown thing like a city built over a fault line. That background dread is unusual in a genre that usually positions its ancient history as epic and glorious rather than ominous and unresolved.

Ye Fan's cultivation technique is drawn from an ancient scripture that most people in the present era consider fictional. Following it puts him on a developmental path that is almost invisible to standard analysis. Other cultivators who assess him reach the wrong conclusions because the framework they use does not account for what he is doing. There are moments where he is sitting in a room with people who could destroy him, and nobody in that room realizes it yet, which creates a low-level tension that the series sustains across long stretches without needing to manufacture external conflict.

The supporting characters who travel with Ye Fan through the early arcs bring a lightness to the series that keeps it from becoming too heavy under the weight of its own atmosphere. The contrast between the cosmic scale of what they are investigating and the very ordinary human bickering that happens among the group is one of the more effective tonal choices the series makes. You can feel genuinely threatened by an ancient sealed ruin and still find the arguments about food and sleeping space funny without those things undermining each other.

Shrouding the Heavens is slower than anything else on this list and more committed to atmosphere over momentum. It is also the most singular. Nothing else in the genre uses space travel and archaeology to frame a cultivation story. Nothing else gives its ancient world the specific quality of dread that this series builds. If that sounds interesting rather than exhausting, the investment pays off in a way that stays with you.

Swallowed Star

Sci-Fi Cultivation · Post-Apocalypse · Cosmic Scale · Long Progression

Swallowed Star is the series I recommend to people who think they do not like cultivation donghua, because the first arc does not feel like cultivation donghua. It feels like a post-apocalyptic survival story. Earth has been overrun by monsters. Human civilization survives in fortified cities. The people who can operate outside the walls are warriors who have pushed their bodies far beyond normal limits through sheer physical training. There is no magic, no ancient scripture, no sect politics. Just a ruined world and the question of how long you can survive in it.

Luo Feng trains obsessively not because of destiny or bloodline but because his family's safety depends on his becoming strong enough to earn money in the dangerous zones outside the city. That grounded motivation makes his early development feel different from most cultivation protagonists. He is not seeking power for its own sake. He is solving a specific, practical problem, and the strength he builds is a byproduct of that rather than an end in itself. It gives the early episodes an urgency that more traditionally framed cultivation stories sometimes lack.

The world expands outward in stages that the series handles with unusual care. The city walls open into the ruins of Earth. The ruins of Earth reveal that humanity has had contact with an interstellar civilization for longer than anyone living knew. That interstellar civilization turns out to be one small part of a universe containing multiple tiers of power that make Earth's entire history look like a footnote. Each revelation reframes the previous one without making the earlier stages feel meaningless, which is a difficult thing to manage across hundreds of episodes.

The cosmic civilizations Luo Feng eventually moves through are developed with real specificity. They have histories, internal politics, competing philosophical schools about the nature of cultivation and consciousness, and relationships with each other that predate humanity's existence. When Luo Feng enters this larger stage, the series is honest about how marginal he is in it. He is genuinely weak relative to the forces around him for long stretches, and his progression through those stages requires patience with a slower pace than the Earth-based arc delivers.

The concept of mind and body cultivation in Swallowed Star is handled in more depth than most series attempt. Luo Feng's particular path involves developing spiritual consciousness alongside physical strength, eventually to the point where the two can operate separately. The implications of that, practically and philosophically, become increasingly interesting as the series moves into its later arcs. It is the kind of world-building detail that rewards sustained engagement because the series returns to it and expands on it rather than introducing it once and moving on.

Swallowed Star asks for a longer commitment than anything else here and keeps delivering further out than most viewers expect. The Earth-based arc is the easiest entry point, but the series gets more ambitious the longer it runs, not less. For anyone willing to follow where it goes, watching someone grow from city survival toward genuine cosmic significance across hundreds of episodes is unlike anything else the genre is doing right now.

06 of 07

Immortality (Yong Sheng)

Dark Cultivation · Sect Politics · Slow Climb · Defying Fate

Most cultivation stories gesture at how unfair and hierarchical their worlds are before moving on to a protagonist who somehow rises above that unfairness fairly quickly. Immortality is one of the few that actually commits to what that unfairness feels like from the inside and stays with it long enough to make it mean something.

Fang Han enters a sect as a foundation-building disciple, which is as low as you can be while technically still being a member. The sect runs on a strict economy of resources. Pills, techniques, training materials, access to better practice grounds, all of it flows upward toward disciples with the right bloodlines, the right mentors, or the right level of demonstrated talent. Fang Han has none of those. He has a physical constitution that most assessors mark as mediocre and no family connection worth mentioning. The sect admits him because the process requires it, not because anyone expects anything from him.

The early episodes spend real time inside the routine of that position. Fang Han cleans. He carries things. He does the labor that keeps the sect's operations running while inner disciples train and advance. This is not glamorous viewing, but it is purposeful. The series is establishing exactly what the walls of his situation look like before it starts showing you how he finds cracks in them. Understanding the constraint makes the eventual evasion of it feel specific rather than generic.

The cultivation technique Fang Han develops is not recognized by the sect's existing framework. He pieces it together from fragments, from texts in the sect library that nobody else bothered with because the mainstream consensus says they lead nowhere useful. His development looks like nothing to the people around him because they are measuring it with tools that were designed for a different path. This creates a constant gap between what others see when they look at him and what is actually happening, a gap that becomes increasingly dangerous as the stakes around him rise.

The sect politics in Immortality are drawn with unusual specificity. Factions compete for influence over the sect leadership, which controls which cultivation lineages receive institutional support. Senior disciples leverage junior ones as resources in their own conflicts, and the informal power dynamics between them are more important than the official hierarchy in determining who actually advances. Fang Han has to understand this system before he is strong enough to ignore it, and the series shows that navigation in detail rather than simplifying it into a clean narrative of corruption versus virtue.

Immortality is genuinely patient in a way that not all viewers will appreciate. The first quarter of the series asks you to sit with someone who is not visibly impressive and trust that what is building underneath will eventually surface. For viewers who are specifically interested in how cultivation worlds actually work as societies rather than simply as settings for power progression, this is one of the most considered takes on the genre. The patience it asks for is the same patience it is trying to show you is necessary to survive in a world that does not hand anyone anything.

07 of 07

Renegade Immortal

Dark Cultivation · Harsh World · Transformation · Relentless Will

I kept coming back to Renegade Immortal in my head for weeks after starting it, and not because I was happy about how things were going for Wang Lin. The series does something uncomfortable that most cultivation stories avoid by design: it shows you a person being changed by their circumstances in ways that are not improvements, and it refuses to frame that as tragic. It just shows you what happens to someone who survives a world that was never going to be fair to them.

Wang Lin starts with nothing that the cultivation world values. His talent assessment comes back as essentially zero. His family has no cultivation history, no connections to sects, no resources worth mentioning. He gets into a sect by what amounts to accident during a broad recruitment sweep, and the sect's response to his presence ranges from indifference to contempt. He is placed at the bottom of every hierarchy and given the worst of everything. The series stays in this position with him for long enough that you start to understand, in a way that most cultivation stories only gesture at, what it actually costs to keep going when the system is designed to produce your failure.

The early stages of Renegade Immortal are deliberately slow. There is no early indicator that Wang Lin is secretly special, no ancient master who notices hidden potential, no lucky discovery that accelerates his timeline. He gets stronger by surviving situations he should not have survived, and each survival changes him in ways that make the next situation slightly more manageable. It is incremental in a way that can be frustrating if you are watching it quickly, but watching at the pace the series intends, it builds a portrait of accumulation that becomes genuinely moving.

The cultivation world in Renegade Immortal is not organized around justice or merit. Sects accumulate resources and control access to them. Powerful individuals enforce arrangements that benefit the powerful. Wang Lin learns repeatedly that protection from stronger parties comes at costs that are not always stated upfront, and that the people most willing to offer help are often the ones with the most calculated reasons for doing so. Each time he learns this lesson the series shows you how the learning changes him, how much of his original warmth gets worn down and replaced by something harder and more efficient.

The relationship Wang Lin carries for his family throughout the series is the emotional thread that keeps you invested even as the character becomes increasingly difficult to read. He does not talk about them often. He does not make decisions by explicitly invoking them. But they are the reason he originally pushed past his assessment, and their presence in his motivations remains traceable even when everything else about him has transformed. The series handles this subtly rather than sentimentally, which makes it land harder than a more obvious treatment would.

As Wang Lin accumulates genuine power, there is not much to celebrate in the conventional sense. He is effective. He has outlasted and outmaneuvered enemies who were stronger and better resourced at every stage of his development. But you watched what it cost him to become that, and the series does not pretend the cost was worth it in any clean way. Renegade Immortal is the series on this list that stays with you longest and makes you think hardest about what cultivation fantasy is actually saying about ambition, survival, and what a person sacrifices to keep going.
