India's Big Bet:
Three Films That Could
Redefine Indian Cinema

After Baahubali and RRR changed how the world looked at Indian cinema, three projects are now being built to push that further. How they perform will matter beyond box office.

Essay
Indian Cinema · Upcoming

What Baahubali and RRR actually changed

Before Baahubali, Indian cinema was broadly respected in its home markets and broadly ignored everywhere else, with occasional exceptions at festivals or through diaspora audiences. Baahubali did not just succeed commercially. It demonstrated that an Indian film made entirely on Indian terms, rooted in Indian mythology and scale and emotion, could hold a global audience without apologising for any of it. RRR then confirmed that was not a fluke.

The shift was less about the films themselves and more about what they proved was possible. Indian cinema had long been capable of scale and ambition. What changed was the confidence to match that ambition with technical quality and then trust the result on an international stage. Both films went wide, both were received seriously, and both made it harder to dismiss Indian cinema as a niche product for a specific audience.

That is the moment three films in development are now being built inside. They are not riding the wave blindly. They are making very deliberate choices about what kind of cinema to make next, and those choices say something about where Indian filmmaking thinks it can go.

Baahubali and RRR did not ask for permission to be taken seriously. The films that come next have to prove that confidence was not a one-time thing.
Between the Frames
Film 01
Ramayana
Epic · Mythology · IMAX

The Ramayana has been adapted hundreds of times across Indian storytelling traditions, on stage, on television, in animation, in regional cinema. Every Indian audience already has a version of it living in their head. That is both the advantage and the problem. The film cannot tell this story as if it is new. It has to offer a version that earns the right to exist alongside all the ones people already carry.

The creative team assembled around it signals serious intent. A.R. Rahman and Hans Zimmer working together on the score is not a novelty pairing. Rahman has spent decades writing music that carries specific cultural weight without becoming culturally closed. Zimmer understands how to score at cinematic scale. Between them the expectation is a soundtrack that can hold both audiences: the one for whom the Ramayana is sacred, and the one encountering it for the first time.

Visual effects handled by DNEG, which has worked on films at the top of international production, points to a decision to compete technically without compromise. This is not the approach of a film hoping to be impressive given its budget. It is the approach of a film that does not want budget to be the conversation at all.

The actual test is narrative. Films built around mythological material this familiar to one audience and this unfamiliar to another tend to split down the middle. They either over-explain and lose the people who know it, or assume knowledge and lose the people who don't. Getting that balance right is harder than any production challenge, and nothing in the trailer can tell you whether they have managed it.

Film 02
Varanasi
Scale · Symbolism · SS Rajamouli

After RRR, there was a reasonable question about where Rajamouli could go next. The film had pushed action spectacle and emotional scale about as far as live action allows in a three-hour runtime. Varanasi is the answer, and from what is known about it, the direction is inward rather than outward.

Varanasi as a setting carries meaning that cannot be separated from the city itself. It is one of the oldest continuously inhabited cities on earth, a place built entirely around the relationship between the living and the dead, where the river is not a backdrop but the reason for everything. A film set there is not choosing a location. It is choosing a philosophy.

The risk with a film that carries this much symbolic weight is losing the story inside the meaning. Rajamouli's filmmaking has always been rooted in characters whose emotions are legible even when the scale around them becomes impossible. If Varanasi holds that quality, the setting amplifies everything. If it loses that thread, the symbolism becomes decorative. His track record suggests he knows the difference.

Film 03
Baahubali: The Eternal War
Animation · Theatrical · Universe Expansion

Indian animation has been consistently underestimated, by the industry itself as much as by audiences. The default assumption is that animation means content aimed at children, or content that supplements a live-action property without carrying real weight. Baahubali: The Eternal War is a direct challenge to both assumptions.

It is being built as a theatrical film, not a streaming spinoff. That decision matters because it forces the work to be judged by theatrical standards: visual clarity at large scale, narrative that sustains itself without live-action anchoring, emotional investment built entirely through style and movement. These are harder things to achieve than they sound, and choosing to pursue them in a theatrical frame rather than a safer streaming release is a statement about what the project thinks it can be.

The Baahubali universe already has audience investment built in, which gives the film a foundation it would otherwise need years to construct. The question is whether the animation can translate the specific texture of that world, its grandeur, its mythic register, its emotional directness, into a form that feels like an expansion rather than a reduction. If it does, it changes what Indian animation is allowed to attempt. That is a more significant outcome than any single film's performance.

What these films are actually betting on

None of these are safe projects. Each one is operating in territory where the expected outcome is uncertain and the consequences of falling short are significant. Ramayana carries religious and cultural weight that makes every creative choice contestable. Varanasi is asking an audience that came for RRR's kinetic energy to follow into something more interior. Baahubali: The Eternal War is asking Indian animation to be taken as seriously as Indian live-action filmmaking.

What connects them is that none are chasing what already worked. They are not sequels to RRR or Baahubali made to capture the same audience with the same formula. They are each trying to open a different door, and the doors they are trying to open are ones Indian cinema has not yet walked through at this scale.

The global film industry right now has a specific kind of appetite. Audiences have grown tired of franchises that repeat themselves and stories that feel engineered for maximum inoffensiveness. There is real interest in cinema that comes from somewhere specific, that carries a perspective shaped by its own culture rather than smoothed away for universal consumption. Indian cinema is better positioned to offer that than almost any other industry in the world.

Whether these three films deliver on that positioning is something only the finished work can answer. But the ambition itself is already a statement.
