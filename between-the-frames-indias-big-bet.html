India's Big Bet: Three Films That Could Redefine Indian Cinema

How Ramayana, Baahubali: The Eternal War, and Varanasi reflect a shift in ambition, scale, and intent.

The Moment Indian Cinema Is Building Toward

Something has shifted in Indian cinema. Not just in box office numbers or stardom, but in the confidence behind what is being made. For decades, Indian films operated within familiar structures. Regional industries evolved independently, while international recognition remained limited to specific festivals or rare crossover moments.

Then came Baahubali. Then came RRR. Both films did more than succeed commercially. They changed how Indian cinema was perceived outside its home markets. These were not films seeking validation. They arrived with conviction, scale, and clarity about what they wanted to be.

Now, that confidence is being tested again. Three projects currently in development, Ramayana, Baahubali: The Eternal War, and Varanasi, point toward the next phase. These are not incremental expansions. Each represents a deliberate attempt to push Indian cinema into new territory while staying rooted in its own identity.

Why This Moment Matters

The global film industry is unstable. Familiar franchises are losing momentum, and audiences are increasingly resistant to repetition. There is growing interest in stories that feel specific, grounded, and culturally confident rather than engineered for universal appeal.

Indian cinema enters this moment with a rare advantage. It carries centuries of narrative tradition, a wide range of visual languages, and an audience base comfortable with scale and emotion. What has changed is the willingness to match that storytelling confidence with technical ambition and long-term investment.

These three films are not guarantees of success. They are tests. They ask whether Indian cinema can sustain global attention without diluting its voice, and whether scale can be paired with discipline rather than excess.

Ramayana

Ramayana is not being treated as a routine adaptation. It is positioned as a large-scale cinematic undertaking designed for IMAX presentation, with extensive international collaboration involved in its execution. The source material itself carries immense cultural and spiritual weight, and that reality shapes every creative decision around the project.

The involvement of A.R. Rahman alongside Hans Zimmer signals an intent to approach the material with seriousness rather than novelty. Rahman’s history of grounding large compositions in cultural texture meets Zimmer’s experience in shaping cinematic scale. The collaboration is less about spectacle and more about constructing a soundscape capable of carrying the narrative weight the text demands.

Visual effects work is being handled by DNEG, a studio associated with large-scale international productions. This choice reflects an expectation that Ramayana should meet the highest technical standards available, not as an experiment, but as a baseline requirement.

The challenge is clear. Ramayana must speak to audiences deeply familiar with its meaning while remaining coherent to viewers encountering it for the first time. Achieving that balance will determine whether the film stands as a landmark or a cautionary example of ambition exceeding control.

Varanasi

Varanasi occupies a different but equally ambitious space. It does not step away from scale or symbolism. Instead, it appears focused on combining physical environments, mythic undertones, and cinematic realism within a single framework.

The project arrives in the wake of RRR’s international reception, and it reflects an understanding that global audiences respond to clarity of emotion as much as scale. Varanasi does not position itself as an imitation. It positions itself as a continuation of a broader conversation about how Indian stories can travel without losing grounding.

The risks here are structural rather than technical. A film that balances realism, symbolism, and scale must maintain narrative control throughout. If it succeeds, it strengthens the case for Indian cinema as a space where ambition and restraint can coexist.

Baahubali: The Eternal War

Baahubali: The Eternal War represents a shift in medium rather than scale. By expanding the Baahubali universe through animation, the project challenges long-held assumptions about the limits of Indian animated cinema.

This is not positioned as supplementary material. It is designed as a theatrical experience, using stylized visuals and expressive movement to explore parts of the Baahubali world that live-action filmmaking would constrain.

The strength of the Baahubali identity provides a foundation, but it also raises expectations. The project will be judged not only on its animation quality, but on whether it extends the emotional and narrative weight of the original films in a meaningful way.

If successful, it expands the definition of what Indian cinema can be. If it fails, it still marks an important moment where animation was treated as a serious cinematic form rather than a secondary one.

What Is at Stake

These projects are not only about individual success or failure. They influence how future investments are made, how risks are evaluated, and how confidently Indian cinema can operate on the global stage.

Ambition at this scale invites scrutiny. It also invites opportunity. Whether these films meet expectations or fall short, they signal a willingness to move beyond safe repetition.

For Indian cinema, that willingness may prove as important as any box office result.
