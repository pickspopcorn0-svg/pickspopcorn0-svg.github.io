Editorial • Indian Cinema

Indian Cinema
at Full Throttle

These films don't ease you in. They commit completely, from the first frame to the last, with a kind of conviction that is difficult to manufacture. Indian cinema at its best operates at a frequency most global cinema won't touch.

5 Films Epic Committed Bold Unapologetic

That's exactly the point. These five films are the clearest argument for why this cinema matters and why its refusal to be modest is a strength, not a flaw.

01 of 05

RRR

2022 • S.S. Rajamouli • Epic Action

RRR is the film that changed how the world talks about Indian cinema. It won an Oscar. It got standing ovations at international festivals. It made people who had never watched a three-hour Indian film sit through every minute and immediately search for what to watch next. S.S. Rajamouli made a film so committed to its own emotional logic that skeptical audiences walked out as true believers.

The story is about two men who become brothers before discovering they are on opposite sides of a war. Rajamouli understands better than almost any filmmaker alive that spectacle only works when it's in service of emotion. The fire and water imagery running through the film is not decoration. Fire is Ram: disciplined, contained, dangerous under control. Water is Bheem: instinctive, powerful, impossible to stop once it moves.

Naatu Naatu, the song that won the Oscar, is the film in miniature. Two men dancing in a British colonial palace, performing a folk dance that is physically punishing and culturally specific, right in front of the people who have stolen their country. It's defiance as joy.

The second half lands so hard because the first half built something so strong. Every betrayal, every choice, every moment of anguish hits with full weight because you've watched these two men become brothers. RRR earns its tragedy. It earns its catharsis.

02 of 05

Animal

2023 • Sandeep Reddy Vanga • Psychological Action

Animal is the most divisive Indian film of the last decade. Half the audience believes it is a masterpiece. The other half believes it is irresponsible. Both reactions are valid responses to the same film, which is exactly what director Sandeep Reddy Vanga intended. He knows precisely what he made. The provocations are not accidents. They are the point.

Ranvijay Singh is a man whose entire personality was built around his father's emotional absence. His father was successful, powerful, and never present in any meaningful way during his childhood. Ranvijay responded to that rejection not with distance but with obsession. He doesn't want approval anymore. He wants to destroy everything that threatens the man who never loved him properly.

Ranbir Kapoor gives the most committed performance of his career here. Ranvijay is possessive, controlling, and absolutely convinced that his love for family justifies every choice he makes. Kapoor plays this without vanity or the usual signals that tell the audience not to take the character at face value.

Animal doesn't offer redemption or moral clarity. It ends exactly as it should, with the cycle intact and unbroken. Some find this nihilistic. Others find it the most honest depiction of a certain kind of toxic devotion they've ever seen on screen.

03 of 05

Kill

2024 • Nikhil Nagesh Bhat • Action Thriller

Kill is 99 minutes of sustained, claustrophobic violence, and it is one of the finest pure action films India has ever produced. The concept is stripped to its skeleton: a moving train, a large group of armed criminals, and one army commando who decides none of them are getting off alive.

What makes Kill exceptional is its honesty about physical consequence. These fights don't feel choreographed in the traditional sense. They feel exhausting. Amrit takes serious damage in every encounter. He bleeds, he slows down, his movements become labored. Each fight carries the marks of the previous ones.

The train setting is used with real spatial intelligence. Narrow corridors remove running room and force every encounter to happen at close range with limited visibility. The editing is disciplined enough to track the geography without losing the chaos.

The villain Fani, played by Raghav Juyal, is the film's greatest asset. Juyal is a trained dancer and it shows in the way he moves: fluid, unpredictable, eerie in a way that larger and more physically imposing villains rarely manage.

04 of 05

Kalki 2898 AD

2024 • Nag Ashwin • Sci-Fi Mythology Epic

Kalki 2898 AD is Indian cinema attempting something it has never fully committed to before: a mythology-rooted science fiction epic built at massive scale. Director Nag Ashwin spent years constructing this world, and the ambition is visible in every frame.

The premise fuses Hindu cosmology with dystopian science fiction. In 2898, Earth is nearly dead. A corrupt authoritarian regime controls the remains of civilization from a gleaming citadel city while the rest of humanity scrapes through a vast wasteland. The myth of Kalki, the tenth avatar of Vishnu prophesied to arrive at the end of the Kali Yuga to restore balance, becomes the organizing framework.

What makes the film notable is how seriously it takes its own mythology. The religious and philosophical framework is the actual logic of the story. Characters believe in cosmic cycles, in prophecy, in the idea that destruction and renewal are part of the same process.

The film has real flaws. The hero's entry scene is stretched thin and the forced comedy feels out of place. The pacing stumbles in the middle act. But as proof that Indian cinema can build myth-rooted franchises with genuine scale and ambition, it marks an important step forward.

05 of 05

Dhurandhar

2025 • Aditya Dhar • Spy Action Thriller

Dhurandhar is a 3 hour and 34 minute spy epic that infiltrates Karachi's criminal underworld with the kind of patience most action films don't have. Director Aditya Dhar, who made URI: The Surgical Strike, returns with something far more ambitious and morally complicated.

Ranveer Singh plays an undercover Indian intelligence agent who slips into Karachi and rises through the ranks of its most dangerous criminal syndicates and political networks. The mission is to dismantle the ISI-underworld nexus from within. The film draws loose inspiration from real geopolitical events including the IC-814 hijacking, the 2001 Parliament attack, and the 26/11 Mumbai attacks.

What makes Dhurandhar distinctive is its structure. The film is told in chapters, each one peeling back another layer of the criminal and political ecosystem. You watch the protagonist become part of the machine he's trying to destroy. The film doesn't rush this.

The action sequences are brutal and grounded. The film earns its 18+ rating with disturbing violence. These moments are not meant to entertain. They're meant to disturb, and they do.

Dhurandhar is the first part of a two-part saga. The second film, subtitled "The Revenge," is set to release in March 2026. As the opening chapter of something larger, it's a bold statement about the scale and ambition Indian spy thrillers are now reaching for.
