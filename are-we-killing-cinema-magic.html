Are We Killing Cinema's Magic
Before We Enter the Theatre?

We watch the trailer, then the clips, then the reels, then the breakdowns. By the time the lights go off, the film is already half familiar.

Essay
Cinema · Culture

We used to walk in blind

There was a specific feeling that used to come with going to the cinema. You'd seen a poster, maybe a short teaser, and you had a general idea of what the film was about. But that's where it stopped. You didn't know which scene everyone would gasp at. You didn't know which moment would make the whole theatre go quiet. You didn't arrive with a mental list of things to look out for. You just sat down and let it happen.

That feeling is genuinely rare now. Not because films have gotten worse, but because we are meeting them far too early. A film comes out and by the time most people buy a ticket, they've already watched three trailers, seen a dozen clips, scrolled through reaction videos, and absorbed a week of discourse about whether it's good or not. The film becomes familiar before anyone sits in a seat.

So the theatre experience isn't really the first time anymore. It's more like the final chapter of something you've been reading in pieces for months.

What trailers actually used to do

A good trailer was a door. It opened just enough to show you there was something interesting inside, without telling you the layout of every room. It gave you tone, a feeling, maybe a single image or line that stuck in your head. It made you curious without satisfying the curiosity.

Most trailers now work differently. They give you the emotional arc. They show you the relationship forming and then straining. They show you the moment of crisis and often a glimpse of resolution. They include the funniest joke, the most striking visual, the line that will become a quote. By the end of two and a half minutes, you've watched a compressed version of the film's whole emotional journey.

The result is that you don't enter a film with curiosity. You enter with expectations. You're not watching to discover, you're watching to confirm. And when a scene arrives that you already saw in the trailer, something in the brain shifts from experiencing it to recognising it. Those two things feel very different. Recognition is weaker.

You stop reacting to what's on screen and start checking it against what you already saw. That small shift is enough to reduce the impact of even a great scene.

The moment you stop living the scene

Here's how it shows up in practice. A scene begins and you can feel yourself leaning forward, not because you're inside the moment, but because you remember it. You think: this is that scene. The one from the trailer. The one that was everywhere last week. The one people kept quoting. And suddenly you're not in the film, you're watching yourself watch a scene you already know.

It's a strange split. You're physically in the theatre but mentally you're somewhere slightly outside the story, comparing what's happening to what you expected. The cinema becomes a place of confirmation rather than discovery. The film isn't unfolding, it's arriving exactly where you were told it would arrive.

Big moments still land. The filmmaking is still doing its job. But the emotional ceiling is lower because your brain spent weeks quietly preparing for this. The surprise is gone, and surprise is not a small thing.

Surprise is not about twists

When people talk about surprise in cinema, they usually mean plot twists. But that's a narrow version of what surprise actually does. Surprise is really about the state your attention is in. When you don't know what's coming, you watch differently. You're more open, more present, quicker to be moved. You don't have your guard up because there's nothing to guard against. Cinema needs that openness to do its best work.

The scenes people carry with them for years are often the ones that arrived unannounced. Not necessarily the loudest moments, but the unexpected ones. The moment that came from a direction no one saw. The laugh that caught the whole room at once. The silence that fell over everyone at the same time. That collective experience is what makes watching a film in a theatre different from watching it at home, and overexposure chips away at the conditions that make it possible.

When you know a beat is coming, the beat can still hit. But the body braces. The mind prepares. What should feel like falling instead feels like stepping down deliberately, one foot at a time.

Horror gets hurt most visibly

Horror is the genre where this damage is most obvious because the whole mechanism of horror depends on timing and uncertainty. A scare works because your attention is somewhere else when it arrives. A reveal works because the film has been carefully pointing you in the wrong direction. A sense of dread builds because you genuinely don't know what's behind the door.

Modern horror marketing tends to sell exactly those moments. Jump scares end up in trailers. Creature designs get revealed in promotional material. Suspense sequences become social media clips. And by the time the film opens, audiences have already been scared by the scare, already seen the creature, already lived through the sequence in a compressed thirty-second version. Inside the theatre, none of it lands the same way.

This is why so many people leave horror films saying nothing felt scary. The fear was already spent. It happened on a phone screen weeks before opening night.

Avoiding it is harder than it should be

The obvious answer is to just skip the trailers. But staying uninformed about a film you're excited about has become genuinely difficult. Clips autoplay before you can scroll past them. Thumbnails on streaming platforms reveal images from scenes you haven't reached. The discourse around a film spreads faster than the film itself, and it travels through feeds, comments, group chats, and recommendation algorithms without needing you to search for any of it.

Most people eventually give in. They watch the trailer because it's playing before something else. They see a clip someone shared. They read two sentences of a review. Each piece feels small, but they accumulate. And by opening weekend the emotional roadmap is already drawn even for people who were trying to stay clean.

But sometimes, by accident or effort, you go in with none of it. You miss the trailer cycle. You stay off the discourse for a few weeks. You sit down knowing only the title and a vague sense of what the film is about. And something different happens. Even if the film is imperfect, the experience of actually meeting it for the first time, in a theatre, in the dark, feels different from anything you've had in a while. Fuller, somehow. More like what cinema is supposed to feel like.

In a world where everything is previewed and explained before you experience it, going in blind has become the most unusual thing you can do.

This isn't about getting rid of trailers

Trailers exist for real reasons. There's more content than anyone can keep up with, and people reasonably want some basis for deciding how to spend their time and money. A teaser that gives you the tone of a film is genuinely useful. Not everything deserves blind faith.

But there's a real difference between a trailer that opens a door and a marketing campaign that essentially gives you the whole house. The problem isn't that trailers exist. It's that the appetite for pre-release content has grown to the point where a single trailer is never enough. There are teaser trailers for trailers. There are "official clips" that are just scenes from the middle of the film. There are featurettes, interviews, character posters, set photos, and social content that, taken together, add up to something much larger than a preview.

Recognising that cost doesn't require swearing off trailers entirely. It just requires being a little more deliberate. Watching one and stopping. Skipping the clip that someone says is "just a clip." Deciding that the next film you're genuinely excited about is one you'll meet properly.

Mystery is cinema's last luxury

The first time feeling isn't something you can recreate once it's gone. If you've already watched a scene on your phone, you cannot unwatch it. The version of that scene that exists in your imagination before seeing the film, the version that is still open and possible, collapses the moment you see the real thing. You only get one version of not knowing.

Pick one film this year where you go in clean. One director you trust, or one project you've been looking forward to. Skip the trailers for that one. Stay off the discourse for a few weeks. Walk into the theatre knowing as little as possible. Not as a rule about how cinema should always work, just as a reminder of what it can feel like when it's allowed to.

Most films are fine to approach any way you like. But some are worth protecting. Some deserve to be met properly, in the dark, for the first time.
